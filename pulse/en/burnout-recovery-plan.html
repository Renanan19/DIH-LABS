A realistic recovery plan

DIH LABS · Updated July 31, 2026

Written for the common case: you are running on empty and you still have to show up on Monday.

Quick answer: recover in this order — 1) stabilise sleep, 2) rebuild one defended recovery block, 3) change one structural thing at work, 4) verify with your own data over weeks. Doing them in a different order mostly fails, because habit changes cannot outrun a schedule that makes them impossible, and structural changes are hard to negotiate while you are too depleted to argue.

Most recovery advice assumes a resource you do not have: time off, a sympathetic employer, or the option to leave. This plan assumes none of that. It assumes you can change a few things you control, negotiate exactly one thing you do not, and measure whether it worked.

One caveat before starting. Burn-out is defined by the WHO as resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. "Chronic" sets the clock. Something took months to build, and a fortnight of good intentions will not unwind it. Plan in weeks.

Stage 1 — Stabilise sleep (weeks 1–2)

Sleep first, and only sleep. It is the fastest-responding signal, the most measurable, and the one that makes every subsequent change possible. The AASM and Sleep Research Society consensus is that adults should sleep seven or more hours per night on a regular basis.

Two changes, both of which require nobody's permission:

  • Fixed wake time, seven days a week. Anchoring the wake end stabilises the body clock more reliably than trying to force an earlier bedtime.
  • Screens out of the last hour. Light-emitting screens before bed suppress melatonin, delay the circadian clock, lengthen sleep onset and reduce next-morning alertness. Nothing else on this list is this cheap.

Do not add anything else in these two weeks. Adding five habits at once is how people fail in week three. Details in sleep and burnout.

Stage 2 — Rebuild recovery (weeks 2–4)

The defining early feature of burnout is not tiredness; it is that rest stopped giving anything back. Restoring recovery is therefore not a luxury phase, it is the mechanism.

Pick one block per week — half a day is enough — and defend it the way you would defend a client meeting. It must be in the calendar, it must be specific, and it must be genuinely unavailable. "I'll rest when things calm down" is not a plan; things do not calm down.

Add movement in this stage, not the first. WHO guidelines recommend 150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity activity per week, and physical activity reduces symptoms of anxiety and depression. Walking counts. It is also the first thing people drop when busy, which is why it belongs in a stage of its own.

If evenings are wired rather than tired, slow-paced breathing at around six breaths per minute raises vagally mediated heart rate variability. Five minutes, not thirty.

Stage 3 — Change one structural thing (weeks 4–8)

This is the stage people skip, and it is the one that decides whether the first two hold. A systematic review with meta-analysis in BMC Public Health found that low workplace support and low job control are associated with burnout symptoms, and that job support is protective against emotional exhaustion. Those are conditions, not attitudes. Habits do not change them.

Choose one. Not five — one, specified concretely enough that someone else could confirm whether it happened:

Lever Concrete version Ask it of
Hours"No work after 19:30, and no weekend on-call in the same month as a launch."Manager, in writing
Control"I own the sequencing of my own backlog for this quarter."Manager
Support"A weekly 30 minutes where I can escalate blockers without preparing a case."Manager or peer
Scope"These two projects, not five. Name which three are dropped."Whoever assigns work
Interruption load"Two protected half-days a week, calendar-blocked, notifications off."Team

If long hours are the driver, treat it as the health question it is. WHO and ILO estimates associate 55 or more hours a week with an estimated 35% higher risk of stroke and 17% higher risk of dying from ischaemic heart disease compared with 35–40 hours. See long working hours and your health.

Stage 4 — Verify (weeks 4–12)

Recovery is easy to mistake for the good week that follows a holiday. The check is whether the trend holds after you return to normal conditions.

Watch these, in this order of trustworthiness:

  • Does a day off restore you again? The single most informative question, asked weekly.
  • Has sleep onset moved back earlier and stayed there? Not one night — a fortnight's average.
  • Resting heart rate and HRV trend. Over 2–4 weeks only. Single days are noise. See HRV and burnout.
  • Cynicism. The slowest to return. Expect it to lag the physiology by weeks, and do not read that lag as failure.

What to expect, honestly

Sleep and physiological signals usually respond within weeks. Energy follows. The mental-distance dimension — the flatness about work you used to care about — is typically the last to come back, and sometimes it does not fully return in the same role. That is information, not defeat.

Progress is also not linear. A bad fortnight after a good month is normal and does not undo the previous six weeks. What matters is the direction over a quarter.

When this plan is the wrong plan

If low mood or loss of interest covers your whole life rather than just work, has lasted more than two weeks most of the day, or you have any thought of harming yourself — this is a medical situation, not a scheduling one. See a doctor now. In France, the national suicide prevention line is 3114, free and 24/7. The differences are covered in burnout vs depression.
