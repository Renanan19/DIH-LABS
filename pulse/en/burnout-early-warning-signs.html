The early signs of burnout

DIH LABS · Updated July 31, 2026

Ordered by how early they actually show up — not by how dramatic they sound.

Quick answer: the earliest reliable sign is not a symptom, it is the disappearance of recovery — weekends that no longer restore you. Around it, three measurable things tend to drift first: sleep timing gets later, sleep gets shorter, and heart rate variability falls. Cynicism about the work arrives next. Reduced performance arrives last, which is why waiting for it is a bad plan.

Burnout has a public image problem. The version people picture — someone unable to get out of bed, signed off for six months — is the endpoint, not the process. By the time that image fits, the useful window for cheap intervention has been closed for a while.

The World Health Organization classifies burn-out in ICD-11 as an occupational phenomenon resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed, described along three dimensions: energy depletion or exhaustion, increased mental distance from the job (or cynicism about it), and reduced professional efficacy. The word "chronic" is the operative one. Something has been accumulating for months, and it has been leaving traces the whole time.

Signs in the order they appear

1. Recovery stops working

This is the first one, and it is the one people explain away. A weekend used to reset you; now Monday feels the same as Friday. An evening used to shed the day; now the day follows you into bed. Nothing is dramatic enough to mention out loud, which is exactly why it slides past.

The useful question is not "how tired am I?" but "how much does rest give back?" A person under heavy but survivable load recovers on days off. A person heading toward burnout does not, and the deficit compounds week over week.

2. Sleep drifts before it shortens

Bedtime slides later first — the hour after everyone else is asleep is often the only hour that feels like your own. Then total time drops. The joint consensus of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society is that adults should sleep seven or more hours per night on a regular basis, and that regularly sleeping less is associated with adverse health outcomes.

Evening screens make both worse. Light-emitting screens used before bed suppress melatonin, delay the circadian clock, lengthen the time it takes to fall asleep, and reduce next-morning alertness. The late-night scroll is not the cause of burnout, but it is a reliable accelerant, and it is one of the easiest things on this list to change. More on the loop in sleep and burnout.

3. Heart rate variability falls, resting heart rate creeps up

A systematic review in PLOS ONE found that job stress and burnout are associated with reduced heart rate variability, specifically lower parasympathetic (vagal) activity — the branch of the nervous system responsible for the brakes rather than the accelerator.

Two cautions. First, this is a group-level association, not a diagnostic test: no HRV number tells you that you are burning out. Second, HRV is extremely noisy day to day and varies enormously between people, so only the direction of your own multi-week trend carries information. See HRV and burnout for how to read it without spiralling.

4. Cynicism arrives

The second WHO dimension — mental distance from the job, negativism, cynicism — usually shows up after the body has already started complaining. It sounds like flatness about things you used to care about, irritation at requests that used to be routine, and a running internal commentary about how pointless it all is. People tend to read this as a personality change or a bad team. Often it is a load signal.

5. Efficacy drops — last

Output holds up for a long time because effort compensates for capacity. That is precisely the trap: the metric your employer watches is the last one to move. When work starts genuinely taking longer, quality slips, and small decisions become hard, the process is already well advanced.

The context that decides how fast this goes

Individual signals do not exist in a vacuum. A systematic review with meta-analysis in BMC Public Health found that low workplace support and low job control are associated with burnout symptoms, and that job support is protective against emotional exhaustion. Two people with identical sleep and identical hours can be on completely different trajectories because one has autonomy and a manager who absorbs shocks, and the other does not.

Hours matter too, and they matter more than most people admit. WHO and ILO joint estimates associate working 55 or more hours a week with an estimated 35% higher risk of stroke and 17% higher risk of dying from ischaemic heart disease compared with 35–40 hours. That figure is about cardiovascular mortality rather than burnout specifically, but it marks a threshold worth knowing. See long working hours and your health.

What to actually do at the early stage

  • Protect the sleep window before anything else. A fixed wake time and a screen cut-off do more, earlier, than any other single change.
  • Reclaim one real recovery block per week. Not "less work" in the abstract — one specific, defended, unavailable half-day.
  • Move. WHO guidelines recommend 150–300 minutes of moderate activity per week, and physical activity reduces symptoms of anxiety and depression. It is also the intervention people drop first when busy.
  • Use breathing deliberately. Voluntary slow-paced breathing at roughly six breaths per minute raises vagally mediated heart rate variability. Five minutes is a real dose.
  • Change something structural. Sleep hygiene will not out-run a job with no control and no support. If the load is structural, the fix has to be too — scope, staffing, hours, or the role itself.

When an app is the wrong tool

Tracking helps with drift. It does not help with crisis. If you have persistent low mood, loss of interest in everything rather than just work, hopelessness, or any thought of harming yourself, that is a medical situation and it needs a doctor or a mental health professional now, not a chart. Burnout and depression overlap enough that the distinction is a clinical one — see burnout vs depression.
