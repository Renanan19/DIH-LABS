The contrast therapy protocol

DIH LABS · Updated July 15, 2026

Heat. Cold. Rest. How to sequence the three phases into rounds that actually work.

Quick answer: a classic contrast round is 10–15 min sauna → 1–3 min cold → 5–10 min rest, repeated 2–3 times. Beginners shorten every phase; the rest phase is not optional. Hydrate between rounds and stop any session that stops feeling controlled.

Alternating hot and cold is the oldest structured wellness practice in the Nordic world: sauna, lake, bench, repeat. What makes it work is not the extremes — it is the structure. Each phase has a job, and the sequence turns two opposite stressors into one coherent session. This is the protocol Sauna Cycle is built around, and here is how to run it manually or with the app.

The three phases and what each one does

Heat (sauna)

Core and skin temperature rise, heart rate climbs to light-exercise levels, vessels dilate, sweating sets in. The heat phase does the cardiovascular work of the session. Typical dose: 10–15 minutes at traditional sauna temperatures, less if you are new — our guide on how long to stay in a sauna covers the duration question in detail.

Cold (plunge, shower or lake)

A short, sharp counter-stimulus: vessels constrict, breathing needs active control, noradrenaline surges. The cold phase is measured in seconds and low minutes, not tens of minutes — 1 to 3 minutes covers almost everyone. Technique and safety live in the cold plunge beginner's guide.

Rest

The phase everyone skips and nobody should. Sitting or lying down for 5–10 minutes lets heart rate and blood pressure settle and is where the deep post-contrast calm actually arrives. Rest also makes the next round feel like a fresh stimulus instead of an accumulation of fatigue. Wrap up in a towel, drink water, do nothing. That is the protocol.

Hot-first or cold-first? End hot or end cold?

Start with heat. Warm muscles and dilated vessels make the first cold exposure safer and more tolerable than plunging cold from a resting state. This is how the traditional Finnish and Danish sequences run.

Ending is a choice. Finish on cold and you walk out alert and sharp — most people prefer it for morning or midday sessions. Finish on a final rest after heat and the session lands softer — better before sleep. Neither is wrong; pick by what the rest of your day needs.

Frequency and progression

  • 2–3 sessions per week is enough to feel the adaptation build within a month.
  • Progress one variable at a time: first add rounds, then lengthen heat, then lower cold temperature. Never all three at once.
  • Expect the cold to change first. The entry gasp fades, controlled breathing arrives sooner, and immersions that felt violent become routine. That progression is what the Sauna Cycle Thermal Adaptation Score quantifies from your logged sessions.
  • Plateau weeks are normal. Consistency across months beats intensity in any single week.

The rules that never change

  • Hydrate before, between rounds, and after. Contrast sessions lose more fluid than sauna alone.
  • No alcohol. Not before, not during.
  • Never do your cold immersions alone, especially in open water.
  • Dizziness, nausea, chest discomfort or a pounding heart end the session immediately.
  • Heart conditions, blood pressure disorders or pregnancy: medical clearance before your first contrast session, not after.
