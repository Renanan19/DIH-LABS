How long should you stay in a sauna?

DIH LABS · Updated July 15, 2026

The honest duration guide: what the research suggests, what your body signals, and how rounds change the math.

Quick answer: 10–20 minutes per round at traditional temperatures covers most goals. Beginners: 5–10 minutes. Want more heat? Add rounds with rest between them, not minutes to a single sit. And any warning sign — dizziness, nausea, headache — beats any timer.

"How long" is the most searched sauna question and the one with the most nonsense answers. The real answer has three layers: what research associates with benefits, what temperature and humidity do to tolerable time, and what your body says today. Here is each layer.

What the research suggests

In the Finnish cohort studies (Laukkanen et al., JAMA Internal Medicine, 2015), session length was one of the measured variables: men whose typical sessions exceeded 19 minutes showed lower cardiovascular risk than those staying under 11 minutes. That is an association within a lifelong, culturally normal practice — not an instruction to white-knuckle 20 minutes on your first visit. The realistic reading: once adapted, sessions in the 15–20 minute range appear to be where the interesting associations live.

Why temperature changes the answer

Twenty minutes means nothing without the thermometer. A traditional Finnish sauna at 80–100°C loads your thermoregulation far faster than an infrared cabin at 50–60°C, and high humidity (throwing water on the stones) makes the same air temperature feel dramatically hotter because sweat stops evaporating efficiently. Rule of thumb: the hotter and more humid the room, the shorter the round. If you switch between sauna types, judge by how you feel and your heart rate, not by your usual minutes. Steam rooms run on entirely different numbers — see sauna vs steam room.

The body signals that override every timer

  • Dizziness or lightheadedness — the classic sign blood pressure is dropping. Out, now, slowly.
  • Nausea or headache — overheating or dehydration. Session over.
  • You stop sweating despite the heat — a red flag for dehydration.
  • Uncomfortable racing heart — elevated is normal; alarming is not.

None of these are failures. Heat tolerance varies day to day with sleep, hydration, training load and stress. The practice is leaving on time, not lasting longest.

Rounds beat marathons

If your goal is more total heat exposure, the structure that works is several moderate rounds separated by rest — not one long sit. Three rounds of 12 minutes gives you 36 minutes of heat with two recovery windows; one 36-minute sit is a dehydration exercise.
